The first folding phones were arguments disguised as products. They asked you to accept a visible crease, a battery that ran out by mid-afternoon, and a hinge that collected pocket lint like it was being paid to. In exchange you got a tablet that fit in a jacket. For a long time that trade only made sense to people who wanted to be seen making it.

That era is over, and it ended quietly.

## The hinge stopped being the story

Early hinges were mechanical showpieces. Manufacturers published exploded diagrams, counted the gears, and named the mechanism. It was marketing, but it was also an admission: the hinge was the risky part, and everyone knew it.

Current designs have fewer moving pieces and more tolerance built in. The crease has not disappeared, but it has moved from something you notice constantly to something you notice when you look for it. Once a defect drops below the threshold of daily attention, it stops shaping how people feel about the device.

The more interesting shift is that reviewers no longer lead with durability. When the failure mode stops being newsworthy, the category has matured.

## Software caught up second

Hardware got there before the software did, which is the usual order and the usual frustration. For years, unfolding a phone meant watching an app rescale badly, lose its scroll position, or restart outright.

Three things fixed most of it:

- Continuity became a platform guarantee rather than a per-app feature, so state survives the fold.
- Layout systems moved to size classes instead of device lists, which means apps adapt to a shape they have never seen.
- Multitasking on the inner screen stopped imitating desktop windowing and settled on two or three panes with predictable rules.

None of this is glamorous. All of it is why the device now feels like one product instead of two products sharing a battery.

## What the format is actually good at

The pitch was always "a tablet in your pocket," which undersold it. In practice the inner screen wins at a narrower set of tasks than the marketing suggested, and it wins at them decisively:

Reading long documents without a scroll bar that feels like a treadmill. Comparing two things side by side — a spec and an implementation, a listing and a map, a message and the thing it refers to. Handing a screen to someone across a table without them having to lean in.

Those are ordinary tasks. That is the point.

## The cost question has not gone away

Folding phones remain expensive, and the second screen still means a smaller battery than a comparable slab. Repair pricing is improving but is not close to parity. Anyone who tells you the trade-off has vanished is selling something.

What has changed is that the trade-off is now legible. You are paying for a specific shape that suits a specific way of working, not for a demonstration of what is technically possible. That is a much easier purchase to reason about, and a much harder one for a competitor to dismiss.

The party trick became a form factor. It took six years and it looks like almost nothing happened.
